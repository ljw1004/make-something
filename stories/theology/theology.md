  In the Enchiridion, the early fifth-century Christian bishop Augustine uses healing to illustrate the difference between a body and a defect in it. When a wound heals, the body regains health. The wound does not move elsewhere or leave the body as a separate substance. It ceases to exist as the injury is repaired.

  This example helps explain his account of evil: something harmful can be real without being a substance in its own right. Removing the harm restores the thing that suffered it.

  **Justice deserves greater love than wealth**

  For Augustine, the early fifth-century Christian author of City of God, created things can be good without deserving equal love. Gold has value, but justice deserves far greater regard. His explanation of greed depends on this ranking: recognizing the goodness of wealth does not justify sacrificing justice to obtain it. [City of God XII.8](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120112.htm).

  The proper relationship between these goods should shape a person's choices. When gaining gold would require acting unjustly, justice should take precedence. Someone who instead gives wealth priority reverses that relationship. Augustine can therefore locate moral failure in the ordering of a person's love without treating the desired object as evil.

  At the close of the sixth day in the biblical creation account, God surveys everything he has made and finds it “very good” (King James Version). The judgment concerns the whole created order. [Genesis 1:31](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%201%3A31&version=KJV).

  Augustine, a Christian bishop writing in Roman North Africa in the early fifth century, takes creation's goodness as a starting point for explaining evil. In the Enchiridion, he distinguishes the unchangeable goodness of God from the goodness of creatures. Created things need not be equally good, and their goodness does not make them incapable of change. [Enchiridion §10](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1302.htm).

  This distinction leaves room for a good creature to suffer damage. The verse affirms that creation is good; Augustine's further argument explains how creatures can lose some of their goodness without evil becoming a separate substance created by God.

## `willing-doing` Augustine, Hippo, 421 AD — Grace

*Augustine, Hippo, 421 AD*

Peter promises to remain faithful to Jesus, then denies knowing him. Why is a sincere promise not enough? And why does the church baptize infants who have made no such wrong choice?

> NOTE: Source context: Augustine’s grace controversy · the scene is set in 421 and reopens his earlier grace writings, including the 418 treatise.

**Goals**
- Why do we fail to do what we know is right? -- `aided-choice`

  Peter, one of Jesus's disciples, promises that he will remain faithful even if everyone else falls away. He wants to stand by Jesus. Will that resolve be enough?

  ---

  **Peter’s sincere promise exceeds his strength**

  In the Gospel accounts, Peter is a disciple of Jesus who promises to remain faithful even when the other disciples abandon him. Augustine, the Christian bishop of Hippo, reads this promise as an example of the difference between genuinely wanting to do right and being able to carry that intention through.

  When Jesus warns his disciples that they will desert him, Peter insists that he will remain loyal even if it costs his life. His promise shows that he understands what faithfulness requires and wants to give it. [Matthew 26:31–35](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2026%3A31-35&version=KJV).

  Commenting on the parallel promise in John’s Gospel, Augustine explains that Peter knows his own desire but misjudges his strength. Jesus predicts Peter’s denial because he knows a weakness Peter has not yet recognized. The promise need not be dishonest for it to fail: sincerity does not establish how someone will respond under threat. Nor does this failure rule out future faithfulness. Peter has something to learn about the help he needs. [Tractates on John 66.1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1701066.htm).

  **Peter denies the allegiance he meant to keep**

  Peter, one of Jesus’s disciples, promises to remain faithful even at the cost of his life. After Jesus is arrested, however, Peter follows him to the high priest’s house and denies knowing him when people identify him as one of his followers.

  Luke’s Gospel describes three denials. After the cock crows, Jesus turns toward Peter; Peter remembers that Jesus had predicted his failure, goes outside and weeps. The episode connects an act of disloyalty with the painful recognition that an earlier, confident promise has been broken. [Luke 22:54–62](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2022%3A54-62&version=KJV).

  Augustine, the Christian bishop of Hippo, treats Peter’s denial as a real failure of allegiance. Fear overcomes his sincere desire to remain faithful, but explaining his weakness does not excuse what he does. Augustine rejects the suggestion that Peter merely denied knowing Jesus as a man while somehow preserving his allegiance to Christ. The disciple’s words disown the very person he had promised to stand by. [Tractates on John 66.1–2](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1701066.htm).

  ---

  **Wanting the good does not ensure doing it**

  In his Letter to the Romans, the apostle Paul describes a conflict between moral desire and conduct: “For the good that I would I do not.” This is the King James Version’s translation of Romans 7:19. Here, *would* means *want to*: the speaker wants to do good but fails to do it. [Romans 7:14–25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%207%3A14-25&version=KJV).

  The passage concerns God’s law and the power of sin. The speaker recognizes that the law is good and inwardly approves it, yet encounters a contrary power at work within himself. Knowing the command has not removed the resistance to obeying it. The problem therefore goes beyond lacking information about right and wrong.

  Paul’s account moves from this divided willing to a cry for deliverance and then to thanksgiving through Jesus Christ. The passage presents the conflict as a need for rescue: moral understanding and good intentions do not, by themselves, overcome the sin the speaker describes.

  **Infant baptism expresses a need for forgiveness**

  Augustine, the Christian bishop of Hippo, treats the church’s baptism of infants as evidence about the salvation human beings need. Baptism is administered for the forgiveness of sins, yet infants have committed no personal wrongdoing. For Augustine, both claims must be taken seriously.

  His argument begins from the church’s practice and its understanding of Christ as saviour. If infants genuinely receive forgiveness and deliverance through baptism, they must have a need that Christ answers. Their lack of deliberate wrongdoing cannot mean that baptism offers them an empty promise. [On Merit and the Forgiveness of Sins, and the Baptism of Infants I.23–25](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/15011.htm).

  Augustine therefore locates the sin being forgiven in an inherited condition. Through descent from Adam, the first human in the biblical story, infants share in humanity’s fallen state before making any choices of their own. Baptism addresses that original sin. Their inability to make a personal profession of faith does not, in his account, prevent them from receiving grace.

  In the *Enchiridion*, Augustine describes Adam’s wrongdoing as corrupting the human stock from which later generations come. The consequences include mortality, a damaged nature and condemnation. Original sin therefore means more than a tendency to make mistakes: in Augustine’s account, people inherit guilt as well as weakness. [Enchiridion 26–27](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1302.htm).

Their need arises from their origin in fallen humanity, before they can understand or imitate anyone’s actions. Augustine explicitly distinguishes inheriting Adam’s condition from copying his disobedience. Bad examples may influence later conduct, but they cannot explain a need for salvation that already exists at birth. [On Merit and the Forgiveness of Sins I.9–10](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/15011.htm).

  **Grace enables a person’s own willing and action**

  Augustine, the Christian bishop of Hippo, argues that acting well requires God’s inward help. He calls this help grace. It does more than add information about duty: God works within a person’s desires and choices, helping that person both want and do what is right.

  Peter’s repentance illustrates the relationship. The disciple promises loyalty to Jesus, then denies knowing him after his arrest. When Peter remembers Jesus’s warning and weeps, Augustine understands his change of heart as the work of Christ’s mercy. [On the Grace of Christ I.49–50](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/15061.htm).

Baptismal water washes the body by its natural properties, but it also serves as God’s instrument in giving grace, or spiritual life in the soul. For Aquinas, an instrument really helps produce an effect, while deriving that power from the principal agent acting through it. Water does not possess an independent natural power to give grace. Luther describes an authoritative declaration of forgiveness; Aquinas describes an instrument that actually conveys grace. The bread points to that body rather than bringing it bodily into the meal. [On the Lord’s Supper, Second and Third Articles](https://www.uecf.net/books/zwingli-and-bullinger/chapter5.html).

  The meal nevertheless has a public and communal purpose. It remembers Christ’s saving death and expresses believers’ gratitude and unity. Those who profess to belong to Christ’s body, understood here as the community of believers, undertake to live accordingly. Remembering Christ thus includes a commitment to other people. [Third Article](https://www.uecf.net/books/zwingli-and-bullinger/chapter5.html).

  - from chapter `trent-justification`: Marburg: If Christ’s body is present, does the bread remain bread?

  What happens to bread when Christ gives his body?

  For Aquinas, what was bread becomes Christ’s body, though its look and taste remain. For Luther, Christ gives his body *with* the bread. Both affirm his bodily presence.

  *If Christ’s body is present, does the bread remain bread?*

  **Aquinas and Luther affirm bodily presence but differ about the bread**

  Thomas Aquinas, a thirteenth-century theologian, and Martin Luther, a sixteenth-century reformer, both affirm that Christ’s body is present in Communion, the Christian meal of bread and wine. Their explanations differ over whether the bread remains bread.

  In the Summa theologiae, Aquinas distinguishes a thing’s substance, meaning what it is, from its sensible properties, such as appearance and taste. At consecration, the bread’s substance becomes Christ’s body while its sensible properties remain. He calls this change transubstantiation. It explains why the consecrated bread still looks and tastes the same even though, in his account, what it is has changed. [Summa theologiae, Part III, question 75, articles 4–5](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/4075.htm).

  In The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, Luther argues that Christ’s body can be present with bread that remains bread. Christ’s identification of the bread with his body warrants belief in that presence without requiring the proposed change of substance. Their agreement about receiving Christ’s body therefore does not amount to agreement about transubstantiation. [The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, sections 2.35–36](https://www.projectwittenberg.org/etext/luther/babylonian/babylonian.htm).

  ---

  **Aquinas, Luther and Zwingli give three accounts of Communion bread**

  Communion is the Christian meal of bread and wine instituted by Jesus. Thomas Aquinas, a thirteenth-century theologian, and the sixteenth-century reformers Martin Luther and Huldrych Zwingli offer different accounts of how its bread relates to Christ’s body.

  Aquinas teaches that the bread’s substance, meaning what it is, becomes Christ’s body at consecration. Its sensible properties, including appearance and taste, remain. This is transubstantiation: the continuing appearance of bread does not mean that its substance remains. [Summa theologiae, Part III, question 75, articles 2 and 4–5](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/4075.htm).

  Luther affirms that Christ’s body is given with bread that remains bread. He treats Christ’s words identifying the bread with his body as sufficient grounds for bodily presence, without requiring a change of substance. [The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, sections 2.35–36](https://www.projectwittenberg.org/etext/luther/babylonian/babylonian.htm).

  Zwingli understands the bread as representing Christ’s body, which he places in heaven following the Ascension. The meal remembers Christ’s saving death without containing his human body. [On the Lord’s Supper, Second and Third Articles](https://www.uecf.net/books/zwingli-and-bullinger/chapter5.html).

  These accounts differ on two connected matters. Aquinas and Luther affirm bodily presence but disagree about whether bread remains. Zwingli rejects bodily presence in the meal and understands the bread as a sign.

[Large Catechism V, sections 16–18](https://bookofconcord.org/large-catechism/sacrament-of-the-altar/).

They also agreed that Christ is received spiritually through faith and that Communion, the Christian meal of bread and wine, strengthens faith through the Holy Spirit. Agreement about spiritual reception through faith could therefore coexist with disagreement about bodily reception.

*Catholic bishops, Trent, 1547*

Catholic bishops meet at Trent to set out their church’s teaching during the Reformation. God gives the help that makes a person right with him. *Does accepting that help mean earning it? And what can good deeds change afterwards?*

  ---

  **God’s help comes before an adult’s response**

  The Council of Trent, a gathering of Catholic bishops during the Reformation, taught in its 1547 Decree on Justification that becoming right with God begins with God’s initiative. An adult cannot begin this reconciliation through unaided choice. God first awakens and assists the person, making a response possible.

  The decree calls this preceding help prevenient grace. The adult can cooperate with it or resist it: God’s initiative does not make the person’s response meaningless. Yet the ability to respond already depends on divine help. Trent therefore distinguishes a real human choice from an independent human beginning. [Decree on Justification, chapter V](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct06.html).

  This teaching concerns adults preparing to receive justification, God’s act of forgiving and renewing them. Their consent participates in receiving the gift; it does not earn the help that makes consent possible.

  - from chapter `trent-reply`: Neither faith nor deeds earn being made right with God for the first time

  When God first makes a person right with him, it is a gift. Nothing they have done beforehand earns it—not their faith, and not their good deeds.

  ---

  **Neither faith nor earlier deeds earn justification**

  The Catholic bishops at the Council of Trent taught in 1547 that a person’s first justification is an unearned gift. By justification they meant both the forgiveness of sins and an inward renewal through which the person becomes right with God.

  Faith is indispensable to this change, but being necessary does not make it a payment. Neither a person’s faith nor any deeds performed before justification deserve the gift. Trent locates its meritorious basis in Christ’s suffering, rather than in the convert’s achievement. [Decree on Justification, chapters VII–VIII](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct06.html).

  An adult’s belief and repentance can therefore matter without placing God under an obligation to reward them. They belong to receiving what God gives, not to earning a right to receive it.

  - from chapter `trent-reply`: After God makes us right with him, deeds done with his help deepen our inner renewal

  Being made right with God includes being renewed within. After that first gift, good deeds done with God's help deepen the renewal. They increase the righteousness the person has received: their renewed inner life with God.

  ---

  **Good deeds can deepen the righteousness God has given**

  The Council of Trent’s 1547 Decree on Justification treats becoming right with God as both forgiveness and inward renewal. For these Catholic bishops, righteousness is not merely a verdict about a person: it includes a renewed life that God gives and sustains. [Decree on Justification, chapter VII](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct06.html).

  This understanding explains why Trent assigns good deeds a role after justification. As a person practices obedience with God’s help, the righteousness already received can increase. Such deeds do more than provide evidence that renewal has happened; they contribute to its growth. [Chapter X and canon XXIV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct06.html).

  The person does not become independent of grace once this life has begun. Christ continues to empower the believer’s actions. Trent’s claim is therefore that deeds made possible by divine help can deepen an existing gift, not that earlier achievements earned its beginning. [Chapter XVI](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct06.html).

- `luther-fruit` Luther: deeds can train us, but do not make a Christian more holy before God

  For Luther, good deeds train the body and help other people. Faith and love can grow. But deeds do not make someone who is already a Christian more holy before God.

  ---

  **Christian deeds follow righteousness received through faith**

  In Concerning Christian Liberty, the reformer Martin Luther explains why Christians should act well if their deeds do not make them righteous before God. His answer gives bodily discipline and service to neighbours important purposes, while locating the believer’s righteousness in faith.

  Luther illustrates the distinction through a bishop’s duties. A person must already have been appointed bishop before performing the work of that office; doing the work does not itself confer the appointment. Similarly, Christian deeds proceed from someone already made righteous through faith. They do not make that believer “a more sacred person,” in R. S. Grignon’s translation. [Concerning Christian Liberty, Part 3](https://www.projectwittenberg.org/pub/resources/text/wittenberg/luther/web/cclib-3.html).

  This does not mean that Christian life requires no further growth. Luther regards faith and love as imperfect and in need of development. Discipline trains the body, and service meets other people’s needs. These are reasons to act, even though the deeds themselves do not make the Christian holier before God.

  **Why Calvin welcomes a fellow believer’s prayers**

## `trent-reply` Catholic bishops, Trent, 1563 — Reform the devotion or reject it?

background: ![Reformation study — next day](assets/theology-reply-records-desk.webp)

*Catholic bishops, Trent, 1563*

The bishops at Trent defend asking saints to pray and honouring images and relics. They also demand an end to superstition and abuses. Saints served God through their bodies, and those bodies await resurrection.

Calvin's reported mistakes raise a sharper question: *should false claims end the practice, or call for its reform?*

[If skipped] Trent rejects abuses while defending honour for saints' images and remains. Calvin rejects the religious devotion even when the object is correctly identified. Better identification does not settle that disagreement.

> NOTE: Source context: Council of Trent · Catholic bishops, 1563; Calvin’s objections retained.

**Goals**
- (three goals are a lot but okay here because prayer, images and relics each receive a distinct reply)
- Does “through Christ” answer the objection to invoking saints? -- `prayer-disagreement`
- Can instruction correct the misuse of images? -- `panel-still-disputed`

**Items**

- `saints-not-saviours` Ask saints in heaven for prayer through Christ, the only saviour

  Saints in heaven pray for us. Asking their help is good and useful: the benefits come from God through Christ, the only redeemer and saviour.

  Asking Stephen and Paul for prayer need not make them rival saviours. The Church receives this practice from its ancient tradition.

  ---

  **Why Trent encouraged Christians to ask saints in heaven for prayer**

  In a sermon for the feast of Saint Stephen, Augustine, the bishop of Hippo, asked two people who had died centuries earlier to pray for peace in the Church. One was Stephen, who had been stoned to death for his faith. The other was Paul, who had approved of Stephen’s killing before becoming an apostle. Augustine could address them together because he believed both now lived with Christ. He credited Christ with the miracles associated with Stephen. [Augustine, Sermon 316.1, 5](https://wesleyscholar.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Augustine-Sermons-306-340.pdf#page=143).

  In 1563 the Catholic bishops at the Council of Trent appealed to the Church’s long practice of asking saints for help. They taught that the saints pray to God for people on earth and that the help believers seek through their prayers comes from God through Christ, the only saviour. On this understanding, someone who asks Stephen for prayer is asking him to join in seeking a gift that only God can give. [Council of Trent, Session XXV, decree on the invocation of saints](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html).

- `trent-image-honour` Teach whom images show; honour passes to the saint, not the material

  Sacred images teach the stories of faith and the saints' examples. Their meaning should be explained and misleading uses corrected.

  Honour shown before a saint's image is directed to the saint represented. The material itself has no divinity or power. This is why kissing an image or bowing before it can be allowed.

  ---

  **Whom Christians honour when they bow before a sacred image**

  In 1563 the Catholic bishops at the Council of Trent defended the practice of kissing a sacred image or bowing before it. In J. Waterworth’s translation, “the honour which is shown them is referred to the prototypes”. The prototypes are the people shown in the images. A Christian who bows before a picture of Christ adores Christ. A Christian who kisses a picture of a saint pays that person the honour due to someone who lived a holy life. The painting itself has no divine power to answer a request. [Council of Trent, Session XXV, decree on sacred images](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html).

  This also explains why the council made instruction part of a bishop’s duty. A picture of a saint’s life could show worshippers an example to follow, provided they understood whom they were seeing and what that person had done. The bishops were to prevent the display of images that taught religious error. [Session XXV, paragraphs on teaching and abuses](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html).

- `bodies-and-resurrection` Saints’ bodies belonged to Christ and will rise again

  Saints' bodies were members of Christ and dwellings of the Holy Spirit. God will raise and glorify them, and grants benefits through them. They therefore deserve honour.

  That does not excuse superstition or false claims. Abuses must be removed; new relics and miracles require investigation and a bishop's approval.

  ---

  **Why a saint’s body still merits honour after death, according to Trent**

  When the apostle Paul wrote to Christians in Corinth about their sexual conduct, he gave a reason why what they did with their bodies mattered. Their bodies belonged to Christ, the Holy Spirit dwelt in them, and the God who raised Christ would also raise them. [1 Corinthians 6:13–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%206%3A13-20&version=NRSVUE).

  In 1563 the Catholic bishops at the Council of Trent used this account of the body to explain why Christians honoured relics such as the bones of a martyr. This was the body in which the martyr had served Christ, and it was the body God would raise to share in eternal life. The council also taught that God granted blessings through the bodily remains of saints. For the bishops, honouring a saint’s remains expressed what Christians believed God had already done through that person and would do at the resurrection. [Council of Trent, Session XXV, decree on the relics of saints](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html).

- `prayer-disagreement` The Church teaches that we may ask the saints to pray for us through Christ

  Why ask the saints for prayer?

  Augustine asks Stephen and Paul to pray for peace. The Church has long sought such prayers through Christ, our only saviour.

  Calvin asks what authorizes us to address the dead and how they could hear us. He disputes the authority of that tradition.

  *The Church teaches that we may ask the saints to pray for us through Christ*

  ---

  **What Calvin and Trent disputed about asking saints to pray**

  In a sermon on Saint Stephen, the early Christian bishop Augustine addressed Stephen and the apostle Paul directly. He asked them to hear him and to pray for peace in the Church. The Catholic bishops at the Council of Trent defended this inherited practice in 1563. In their account, the saints join their prayers to ours, and whatever help God gives comes through Christ, the sole saviour. [Augustine, Sermon 316.5](https://wesleyscholar.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Augustine-Sermons-306-340.pdf#page=146). [Council of Trent, Session XXV, decree on the invocation of saints](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html).

  The Protestant reformer John Calvin distinguished asking a living neighbour for prayer from addressing someone who had died. Even if Stephen prays for the Church in heaven, how would a particular Christian know that Stephen could hear a request made on earth? In his *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, Calvin asks where God has promised such a hearing or invited Christians to address the dead. Trent’s appeal to tradition gave the bishops grounds to encourage the practice. For Calvin, saying that all help comes through Christ still left the worshipper without divine authority to address Stephen in the first place. [Calvin, Institutes III.20.21, 24](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.v.xxi.html).

- `panel-still-disputed` We can correct mistaken worship while honouring the saint an image truly depicts

  What would teaching the worshippers change?

  Explain who stoned Stephen, and they can see why his attackers should receive no candles. Honour belongs to the saint the image represents. Calvin objects even when the worshipper knows which figure is Stephen and offers the candle to him alone.

  *We can correct mistaken worship while honouring the saint an image truly depicts*

  ---

  **Recognising Saint Stephen in an image settles only part of the dispute**

  The Protestant reformer John Calvin recalled a feast day in his childhood parish when the figures of Saint Stephen and the men who stoned him were dressed alike in garlands and necklaces. According to Calvin, women who saw the decorations took the killers for Stephen’s companions and offered a candle to each figure. He related the incident in his *Inventory of Relics* to illustrate how easily worshippers could mistake the objects of their devotion. [Calvin, Inventory of Relics, concluding recollection](https://www.monergism.com/inventory-relics).

  The Catholic bishops at the Council of Trent taught in 1563 that a sacred image could help people understand a saint’s life. Their principle suggests a remedy for the confusion Calvin described. Explaining the scene would show worshippers which figure was Stephen and why Christians honoured his courage. For Trent, honour paid before Stephen’s image could reach Stephen himself. [Council of Trent, Session XXV, decree on sacred images](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html).

  In his *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, Calvin argues that even someone who knows exactly whom an image depicts has no warrant from God to give it religious honour. The worshippers might learn to recognise Stephen and put their candles before his figure alone. Calvin would still object to the act that Trent permitted. [Calvin, Institutes I.11.9–11](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.iii.xii.html).

- `reform-relic-devotion` Reject false relics while honouring the bodies of saints

  What should become of the supposed relic in Geneva?

  If Calvin’s report is right, the shrine held pumice. A stone has no claim to honour as Peter’s remains. But Peter belonged to Christ and awaits resurrection. Finding a stone leaves that reason to honour his actual remains intact.

  *Reject false relics while honouring the bodies of saints*

  ---

  **How Trent’s rule for relics would apply to the stone in a Geneva shrine**

  The Protestant reformer John Calvin reported that a shrine on a Geneva altar once held an object revered as the brain of the apostle Peter. In his *Treatise on Relics*, Calvin says that while the object was enshrined, people regarded even questioning its identity as blasphemous. When it was examined, he says, it proved to be pumice. The reverence shown to Peter had helped shield a claim about a piece of stone from scrutiny. [Calvin, Treatise on Relics, pp. 223–224](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/treatise_relics.v.html).

  The Catholic bishops at the Council of Trent set out a general rule in 1563. Before a new relic could be recognised, the bishop was to investigate and consult people with relevant knowledge. The reason the council gave for honouring a martyr’s bones was that the martyr had belonged to Christ in life and would be raised by God. Applied to Calvin’s account, that reasoning gives a bishop grounds to reject the stone’s claim to veneration. Finding pumice in one shrine leaves the council’s reason for honouring a saint’s actual remains intact. [Council of Trent, Session XXV, paragraphs on holy bodies and recognition of new relics](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html).

  **Why Trent would reform devotion to relics and Calvin would end it**

  Suppose a bone displayed in a church could be securely identified as the remains of a Christian martyr. For the Catholic bishops at the Council of Trent in 1563, that mattered because this was the body in which the martyr had served Christ and in which the Holy Spirit had dwelt. God would raise that body again. The bishops defended honour for such remains, required examination before new relics could be recognised, and ordered the removal of superstition and financial abuses. [Council of Trent, Session XXV, decree on the relics of saints](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html).

  The Protestant reformer John Calvin objected to the devotion even in the case of a genuine bone. In his *Inventory of Relics* he argues that people who collect and display a martyr’s bones can spend their lives tending to the remains while neglecting to follow the martyr’s example. Paying religious honour to the bone gives a created thing the honour owed to God. Calvin commended burying the dead with respect as they await resurrection. Both Calvin and the bishops could therefore condemn passing off a stone as a saint’s remains. They would still disagree about what Christians ought to do with the martyr’s own bones. [Calvin, Inventory of Relics, opening argument and conclusion](https://www.monergism.com/inventory-relics).

  **The close of the Council of Trent**

  On 4 December 1563, at the final session of the Council of Trent, the decrees agreed under earlier popes were read aloud. The bishops were asked whether the council should close and its decrees be sent to Pope Pius IV for confirmation. They assented. Cardinal Morone, who presided, blessed the assembly and bade the bishops go in peace.

  In the acclamations that followed, the Cardinal of Lorraine asked that those who held to the decrees might receive the mercy and grace of Jesus Christ, with Mary and all the saints praying for them. The bishops had just defended the practice of asking the saints for prayer. Here they joined in such a prayer themselves.

  Their answer, in J. Waterworth’s English translation, was “So be it: so be it. Amen, Amen.” [Read the full account of the council’s close and the acclamations, Session XXV](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/trent/ct25.html#:~:text=ON%20THE%20CLOSE%20OF%20THE%20COUNCIL).
