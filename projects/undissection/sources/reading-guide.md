# Reading Undissection's sources

These are original design notes, not textbook excerpts. The [source index](README.md) owns bibliographic details, license evidence, access status and local reading links. The suggested order connects materials to mechanisms and mechanisms to the continued functioning of a whole body.

For the current grades 8–10 ordinary-action prototype, start with [school scope](school-scope.md). The wider sequence below and its water-and-heat notes preserve earlier author research; they are not a requirement to teach microscopic mechanisms or add those structures to the current game.

## Read for construction decisions

1. **Start with a living cell's needs.** Read OpenStax 1.2, 1.5 and 3.1. A selective boundary makes an inside possible, but exchange and regulation keep it viable. Distinguish a concentration gradient, passive movement down a gradient, and transport that requires an energy source. The game can begin with a usable boundary and exchange problem before presenting a named organ.
2. **Learn four recurring tissue families.** Read 4.1–4.5 together. Epithelial tissue supplies linings, barriers, exchange and secretory surfaces; connective tissue supplies support, connection and varied matrices; muscle supplies force; nervous tissue supplies communication and processing. These are broad families, with crucial specializations. Reuse them in several organs so the player recognizes a material serving a new job rather than memorizing four disconnected definitions.
3. **Build a transport circuit with somewhere useful to go.** Read 19.1 and 20.1, then the diffusion material in 20.3 alongside the accuracy caution below. A pump without a connected route cannot serve distant cells. Valves support directed flow; arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins return it. A capillary network must reach the exchange site. Blood is a connective tissue, and capillary endothelium is a specialized epithelium; the same classifications reappear at different scales.
4. **Connect air movement, exchange and blood transport.** Read 22.3–22.5 as a sequence. Moving air into lungs is ventilation; diffusion across the respiratory surface is gas exchange; transport to tissues is circulation. Respiratory muscles alter thoracic volume and pressure. A large thin moist exchange surface is useful only when air and blood reach it. Keep oxygen delivery and carbon dioxide removal visible as connected functions.
5. **Give cellular work a material and energy budget.** Read 23.7 and 24.1; use 24.2 selectively with the cautions below. Digestion reduces food molecules to absorbable forms, absorption crosses the gut epithelium, and cellular metabolism uses those substances. Oxygen is an electron acceptor in aerobic energy metabolism, not a nutrient that the lungs turn directly into energy. ATP supports cellular work; some energy becomes heat. Avoid teaching a universal exact ATP yield through combination counts.
6. **Separate filtration, recovery and regulated output.** Read 25.4–25.6, then 17.3 and 26.2 for control. A nephron has a blood route and a tubular route; these compartments exchange selected substances without simply becoming one open pipe. Filtration starts the process, recovery returns useful water and solutes, secretion moves selected substances into tubular fluid, and excretion is the final removal from the body. A working model should retain useful material while producing an output, then adjust that output when conditions change.
7. **Return to the whole.** Reread 1.5 with the completed assembly in mind, then use 24.6 and 26.2 to connect heat loss with water balance. A feedback loop needs a variable to monitor, information about that variable, an effector that can change it, and a connection that closes the loop. “Add nerves” is not by itself an explanation of regulation. A stable condition is maintained by ongoing activity; the endpoint should be a functioning system, not a pile of correctly named organs.

## Candidate mechanism checks

These are authoring questions, not adopted gameplay or assessment rules.

| Assembly | Evidence of understanding worth making observable |
|---|---|
| Exchange surface | Can the player explain why thinness and surface area help, and identify which substances cross it? |
| Vessel | Can the player distinguish a wall that withstands pressure from a wall specialized for exchange? |
| Heart and circuit | Does the assembled route pass through both lungs and body and return to the pump? |
| Lung connection | Are ventilation, gas exchange and blood flow all necessary for the stated result? |
| Digestive tract | Does absorbed material cross a boundary before it can supply tissues? |
| Nephron | Are useful substances recovered rather than all filtrate being labeled waste? |
| Feedback loop | Does the response oppose a specified deviation, with information returning through the loop? |

## Accuracy cautions

**Preserved source text is not automatically approved teaching text.** The historical sections are retained faithfully so evidence can be checked; corrections belong in these notes and the eventual story, not silently inside the downloaded originals.

- **Capillary fluid return:** OpenStax 20.3 presents the traditional balance of filtration at one end and reabsorption at the other. Modern treatments emphasize ongoing net filtration in most tissues and return through lymphatics; kidney and intestinal microcirculations have specialized conditions. Do not make universal downstream reabsorption, or a universal numerical split between routes, the game's causal explanation. For this game's scope, diffusion between blood and tissue fluid plus an explicit lymph-return route is adequate. See C. Charles Michel, Thomas E. Woodcock and Fitz-Roy E. Curry, [“Understanding and extending the Starling principle”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32270491/) (2020), DOI [10.1111/aas.13603](https://doi.org/10.1111/aas.13603). Only its bibliographic record and abstract were consulted; no full article was retained.
- **Lactate and energy:** The historical 24.2 text incorrectly identifies lactic acid as the electron acceptor in lactate fermentation. Pyruvate is reduced to lactate while NADH is oxidized to NAD⁺, allowing glycolysis to continue. Do not reproduce the incorrect sentence, treat lactate simply as useless waste, or imply that all ATP production requires oxygen. The same section gives a fixed 36-ATP total; avoid requiring one universal count. The reaction is independently described in [NCBI Bookshelf's *Biochemistry, Aerobic Glycolysis*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470170/). Only a short explanatory passage was consulted; no full text was retained.
- **Homeostasis:** “Negative” describes the direction of feedback relative to the initial change, not an undesirable effect. Positive feedback amplifies a process until an endpoint or external limit; it should not be the generic stabilizing controller. Normal conditions vary within ranges rather than being perfectly fixed. Do not infer that every control system requires the brain: local and endocrine control also exist. Read 1.5's examples critically rather than converting every sentence to a rule.
- **Oxygen and vessel identity:** Pulmonary arteries and veins prevent the shortcut “artery equals oxygenated.” Use direction relative to the heart. Distinguish ventilation from gas exchange and cellular respiration. In ordinary respiratory control, carbon dioxide and associated pH signals matter; “low oxygen directly tells the lungs to breathe” is an incomplete default explanation. See 19.1 and 22.3–22.5.
- **Renal selectivity:** Glomerular filtrate normally contains water and many small solutes, including useful ones. Reabsorption and secretion are essential. ADH changes water permeability of relevant distal nephron/collecting-duct segments; it does not command a generic filter to remove “more toxins.” Excretion includes substances leaving by routes other than urine, including carbon dioxide through the lungs. See 25.4–25.6 and 22.5.
- **Tissue reuse:** An epithelium is not just skin, connective tissue is not just an inert frame, muscle is not only skeletal muscle, and nervous tissue includes supporting glia as well as neurons. A capillary is not simply a miniature thick-walled artery. Keep specialization visible when reusing a tissue family. See 4.1–4.5 and 20.1.
- **Model versus development:** Assembly order is an explanatory choice. Real embryological development involves coordinated differentiation, growth, patterning and interaction; mature structures are not created through a literal recipe of adult tissue tokens. The source set supports anatomical and physiological explanation, not a claim to simulate organogenesis.

## Water and heat prototype

Read [26.2 Water Balance](local/openstax/markdown/26-2-water-balance.md), the posterior-pituitary and ADH portions of [17.3](local/openstax/markdown/17-3-the-pituitary-gland-and-hypothalamus.md), and [24.6 Energy and Heat Balance](local/openstax/markdown/24-6-energy-and-heat-balance.md), alongside the existing collecting-duct material in [25.6](local/openstax/markdown/25-6-tubular-reabsorption.md) and water-absorption material in [23.7](local/openstax/markdown/23-7-chemical-digestion-and-absorption-a-closer-look.md). These sources support the following causal interpretation; this is not a claim that the prototype implements every component.

- **Specify the loss before naming the blood state.** In the proposed situation, ordinary hypotonic sweat loss exceeds replacement, so plasma becomes more concentrated and plasma volume can decrease. Sweat contains salts; it is not pure water. Use “more concentrated blood plasma” as a player-facing description. Thirst or the broad word “dehydration” alone does not establish a particular osmolarity in every situation: changes in salt balance and blood volume also matter. The physiological distinction is supported by the [National Athletic Trainers' Association fluid-replacement position statement](https://www.nata.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/fluid_replacement_for_the_physically_active.pdf); only its relevant explanation was consulted, with no full copy retained.
- **Separate detection, synthesis and release.** Hypothalamic osmoregulatory mechanisms respond to increased plasma concentration. Hypothalamic neurons synthesize ADH; their posterior-pituitary nerve terminals store and release it into the blood. ADH then travels in blood to the kidney. Do not describe the posterior pituitary as manufacturing ADH or an ordinary nerve as carrying the released hormone directly to the collecting duct. Sections 17.3 and 26.2 explicitly connect sensing to this hormonal response.
- **Permeability and gradient do different jobs.** ADH promotes insertion of aquaporin water channels into the apical membrane of collecting-duct principal cells. The surrounding medullary osmotic gradient supplies a driving force for water to leave the tubular fluid; renal blood vessels carry reabsorbed water away. Channels make the route more permeable. They do not pump water, create water, or generate the medullary gradient by themselves. Increased recovery usually gives a smaller volume of more concentrated urine. A useful completion condition requires both a permeable route and a maintained gradient, not ADH alone. See 25.6 and 26.2.
- **Conservation does not repay a deficit.** Kidney recovery returns filtered water that was already in the body. Reducing urine water loss can slow net water loss, but cannot replace water already lost in sweat. If losses continue to exceed absorbed intake and metabolic production, total body water keeps declining even with an appropriate ADH response. Avoid an ending in which adding ADH alone fully rehydrates the body. Sections 25.6 and 26.2 support this distinction.
- **A drink crosses an epithelial boundary.** Swallowing puts water in the digestive lumen. Absorption, mostly in the small intestine, transfers it into the body's internal fluids and circulation; further absorption also occurs in the colon. Water does not need to be chemically digested, and it does not travel from stomach straight to bladder. Distinguish incoming replacement water from kidney reabsorption of existing body water. See 23.7 and 26.2.
- **Close the loop on the measured condition.** Once absorbed water and reduced losses bring plasma concentration toward its regulated range, osmotic stimulation of ADH release declines; collecting-duct water permeability and urine concentration can decrease accordingly. Do not make drinking an unconditional switch that immediately turns ADH off regardless of the remaining deficit or volume signals. The simple concentration-feedback account in 17.3 is sufficient for the prototype; it does not model all anticipatory or volume-dependent regulation.
- **Evaporation supplies the cooling.** Sweating puts water on the skin. Its evaporation takes heat from the skin; dripping sweat gives less cooling than evaporation of that water would provide. Humidity can limit evaporation, so a large visible sweat output is not proof of adequate heat loss. Water replacement supports continued function, but a drink is not itself the mechanism by which sweat removes heat. Avoid universal fixed proportions of heat lost through different routes: environment, activity and clothing change them. See 24.6.

Two additional source cautions matter for this prototype. **Osmolality** is expressed per mass of solvent; **osmolarity** per volume of solution. Section 26.2's introductory definition of osmolality in terms of volume is misleading. Keep the units distinct if using them; the qualitative concentration language above avoids burdening the player with this distinction. The [US National Library of Medicine's osmolar-concentration definition](https://meshb.nlm.nih.gov/record/ui?ui=D009994) distinguishes the two quantities.

Section 25.6 also describes collecting-duct urea transport as active pumping and groups the vasa recta into the countercurrent multiplier. For authoring, distinguish **loop-driven gradient generation**, **urea recycling through transporters**, and **countercurrent exchange by the vasa recta that helps preserve the gradient**. Do not derive a powered urea-pump recipe from that wording, or claim that the detailed inner-medullary concentrating mechanism is completely settled. Jeff M. Sands and Harold E. Layton's [“The Physiology of Urinary Concentration: an Update”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2709207/) explains the roles and remaining uncertainty. Relevant passages were inspected; no full article was retained.

## What the previews add

Silverthorn provides a useful integration lens: trace coordinated processes through a changing situation. Sherwood's publisher description makes the maintained internal environment the shared reason for the organs' work. Zahourek's arm workbook offers constructive anatomy as a spatial precedent. These are design inferences from the limited materials actually accessed, not comparative evidence that one approach is more educational or more enjoyable. Spongelab remains an unverified lead because the supplied page could not be accessed.

The transfer worth aiming for is a player's explanation of a new case: why adding more pump force cannot repair a missing exchange surface, why a filter must recover useful substances, or why assembling every part still fails if the parts are connected incorrectly. Whether the eventual game achieves that requires its own evaluation.
