# School scope: an ordinary action

Author reference for the **grades 8–10 organ-and-system prototype**. The question is how a body can perform an ordinary action, such as lifting a cup, and keep doing useful work. Players reconstruct an explanation using mature structures; the assembly does not represent embryological development, surgery or bringing a body to life. The [design brief](../design-brief.md) owns the particular action and story decisions. The [source index](README.md) owns bibliographic and license details.

## Curriculum grounding

- **[NGSS MS-LS1-3](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ls1-3-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes)** asks learners to support an explanation of interacting body subsystems with evidence. Its clarification connects cells, tissues and organs; its assessment boundary emphasizes relationships among circulatory, excretory, digestive, respiratory, muscular and nervous systems, rather than studying a system's mechanism in isolation. The useful outcome here is an explanation of why movement needs several cooperating systems.
- **[NGSS HS-LS1-2](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/hs-ls1-2-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes)** asks learners to model hierarchical organization and interacting systems. Its examples include nutrient uptake, water delivery and movement responding to nerve signals. Molecular interactions and chemical-reaction mechanisms are outside its assessment boundary. A model connecting muscles, circulation, lungs, digestion and nerves fits that level.

These are scope anchors across middle and high school, not a claim of complete standards coverage or evidence of comprehension. Skeletal supports help explain the chosen action. Skin cooling is an optional future extension for continued activity or warmth, not part of the current lift-a-glass scope. Standards alignment alone cannot establish that a game teaches effectively. Adult reviewers and AI playthroughs can find defects, but cannot establish what students understand; that needs student evidence.

## Starting knowledge and teaching boundary

Assume recognition of bones, muscles, heart, lungs, stomach, brain, blood, food, breathing and sweat. Introduce cells as the tiny living units making up muscles and other body parts where the supply route needs them. A full cell–tissue–organ hierarchy is optional unless the chosen explanation uses those distinctions. Do not assume they know a tendon, absorption, a capillary, an opposing muscle pair or a feedback loop; introduce each useful term alongside its job and an observable consequence.

**Keep the explanatory scale at organs, connections and system jobs.** Required ideas are a joint that permits movement, muscles that pull through attachments, transport of useful substances, exchange with air and gut, nerve messages, with cooling that responds to warming reserved for a possible later extension. Familiar names such as oxygen and sugar are usable materials, without teaching their molecular reactions.

Exclude nephron segments, filtration/reabsorption puzzles, hormone names, membrane channels, ion gradients, osmotic calculations, neurotransmitters, action potentials, muscle filaments, ATP accounting and enzyme pathways. Kidneys may receive a plain overview if relevant to a later story, but are unnecessary to explain this action. Do not retain advanced mechanisms merely because the source library contains them.

## Relationships to preserve

| Relationship | Accessible causal explanation | Boundary and common pitfall |
|---|---|---|
| **Joint → moving bones** | The elbow permits the forearm to bend and straighten. Joint shape constrains how bones move; it does not provide the pulling force. | A ready-made elbow can include its cartilage, fluid, capsule and ligaments. Explain only the feature needed now; do not require players to install each component. A tendon connects muscle to bone; a ligament supports a joint by connecting bones. [OpenStax 9.4](local/openstax/markdown/9-4-synovial-joints.md). |
| **Muscles → tendons → bones** | Contracting skeletal muscle exerts a pull, transferred through tendons to bones on the appropriate sides of the joint. Biceps contributes to bending the elbow; triceps contributes to straightening it. | Muscles do not push bones back. The pair is a simplified example with other muscles helping and stabilizing. Do not imply that every contraction shortens a muscle, that one muscle is always entirely inactive, or that letting go is controlled return. Lowering a load can involve the biceps continuing to pull as it lengthens. [OpenStax 11.1](local/openstax/markdown/11-1-interactions-of-skeletal-muscles-their-fascicle-arrangement-and-their-lever-systems.md); the opening of [pinned 10.4](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-anatomy-physiology/blob/b0058a743bdd2cc08196abaaa7d5469970c0d80c/modules/m46470/index.cnxml). |
| **Heart + vessels → circulation** | The heart pumps blood along connected routes. Blood reaches muscles and returns; the lung circuit is joined through the heart: body → heart → lungs → heart → body. | A route with a pump and no return is incomplete. Tiny vessels at the organs provide exchange sites; the term *capillary* is optional. Chambers, valves and named vessels can remain inside the organ model. Arteries lead away from the heart, veins toward it; those names do not mean oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor. [NHLBI: blood flow](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart/blood-flow); OpenStax 19.1 and 20.1. |
| **Lungs + blood → gas delivery and removal** | Breathing brings air to the lungs. Oxygen passes into blood, which carries it to working tissues; carbon dioxide moves from tissues to blood, then to lungs and out in exhaled air. | Breathing, gas exchange and circulation are different jobs. Lungs do not make oxygen, pump blood or turn air into energy. The airways and breathing muscles can be included in the lung model and its description; individual air sacs and gas chemistry need not become operands. [NHLBI: lungs](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/lungs); OpenStax 22.3–22.5. |
| **Digestion + absorption + circulation → fuel supply** | Digestion breaks food into usable smaller substances. The small intestine absorbs nutrients; for example, sugar crosses its lining into blood. Circulation distributes this supply, and muscle cells use fuel to support their work. | Food in the gut has not yet reached muscle. The stomach does not do all digestion or all absorption. Use sugar as the traced example: many fats first enter lymph, so avoid claiming that every nutrient follows an identical route. Liver processing can be acknowledged without an extra assembly step. Existing stores allow movement between meals: eating need not trigger an immediate new supply before each reach. [NIDDK: digestion and absorption](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/digestive-system-how-it-works); OpenStax 23.7 and 24.1. |
| **Brain + spinal cord + nerves → coordinated action** | Nerve messages connect the brain and spinal cord with muscles. Coordinated signals adjust which muscles pull and how strongly; information returning from the body helps guide the action. | These messages convey information, not the muscle's food or oxygen. A combined nerve pathway may include spinal-cord connections. Name the connection and effect before a result depends on it. Adding a brain never starts life, creates energy or repairs an absent blood supply. [NICHD: nervous-system parts](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/neuro/conditioninfo/parts); OpenStax 4.5. |
| **Working tissues + cooling response → temperature regulation** | Activity produces heat. Temperature information reaches control centres in the brain; warming can increase sweating. Sweat evaporating from skin removes heat. As the warming is corrected, the cooling response can ease. | Evaporation supplies the cooling: visible dripping sweat does not prove effective cooling. Conditions such as humid air can limit it. A single arm raise need not cause sweating; use continued activity or warmth for this extension. Stable temperature is a range maintained by ongoing responses, not a permanently completed state. OpenStax [1.5](local/openstax/markdown/1-5-homeostasis.md) and [24.6](local/openstax/markdown/24-6-energy-and-heat-balance.md). |

For the energy connection, “muscle cells use oxygen and fuel to support continued work, releasing heat” is sufficient. It does not mean oxygen is food or that every brief contraction requires freshly delivered oxygen; avoid universal claims that all energy production needs oxygen. Detailed metabolism stays in the author reference.

The heart's rhythm starts within the heart; nerve signals can adjust its rate and strength. If the story introduces brain-to-muscle control after circulation, it must not suggest that the heart was waiting for a brain to switch it on. The internal pacemaker need not become another operand. [NHLBI: how the heart beats](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart/heart-beats).

## What can remain inside the model

An explanatory model is selective. A supplied joint, lung, digestive tract or nerve pathway may stand for an already functioning structure with smaller parts. Make an omitted connection clear in a short description when the current result depends on it. Do not force every named structure into inventory, and do not imply that omitted structures are biologically unnecessary. A hand gripping a cup, shoulder support and posture can be given capabilities while elbow movement supplies the selected mechanical example.

The authoring test is whether a player can explain a meaningful dependency: why a joint needs a pull; why moving blood also needs lung exchange; why digested food must enter circulation; why a supported muscle still needs coordinated signals; or why sweat must evaporate to cool. These are prospective learning checks, not findings that a prototype already teaches them.
