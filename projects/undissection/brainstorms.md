# Competing approaches

These are design candidates, not assertions of tested enjoyment or physiological recipes. Each comparison gives its reviewers the same audience, interaction format and worthwhile criterion.

## Round 1: what is being reconstructed?

### A — Four tissues, one body

Start with epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue as reusable families. Arrange them into a protective surface, an exchange surface, a pump, flexible tubes and a moving limb. Intermediate structures proliferate. Fit the exchange surfaces to the circulation; join sensing and muscles to coordinated movement; converge the structures into one person who can take a walk.

**Combination angle:** the same material performs different jobs depending on its arrangement and partners. A thin surface joined to a capillary bed enables exchange; contractile tissue around a chamber enables pumping. Intermediate recipes would represent assemblies with explicitly provided support, not magical two-tissue organ growth.

**Worthwhile angle:** the body is not a bag of unrelated organs. Much of its variety comes from reusing a small repertoire in different relationships. A familiar action draws on structures the player made separately.

**Risk:** four jargon-like tiles might become universal ingredients; the player could spend the game naming familiar organs rather than discovering physiology. The walk could merely certify completion.

### B — Make an inside

Begin with a patch of living cells, a selective boundary, fluid and a supply of nutrients. The first enclosed space protects an interior, but its cells now need deliveries and waste removal. Build circulation, connect an air-exchange surface and a gut, then assemble kidney filtration and recovery. Finally connect sensing to the mechanisms that adjust flow, water retention and heat loss. The construction becomes one body that keeps a livable interior while interacting with the outside.

**Combination angle:** solving one problem exposes another. A boundary separates; transport crosses it; a circuit serves distant cells; filtration removes too much until recovery makes it selective. Connections consume separate model modules into a growing whole.

**Worthwhile angle:** being alive depends on maintaining conditions around cells. Eating, breathing, sweating and urinating are related exchanges across a boundary. The gut's contents are not yet inside that protected environment.

**Risk:** the early cell-to-body transition can imply a false evolutionary or developmental sequence. “Stable interior” is abstract unless the player sees concrete consequences. Regulatory arrows can turn into paperwork tokens.

### C — One ordinary minute

Start with support, contraction, sensing and exchange modules. Construct an arm that bends, a route from lungs to working muscle and a way to coordinate movement. Ask the body to stand and walk. Increased demand then gives the player a reason to connect circulation, ventilation, temperature regulation and water conservation. End with a person drinking a glass of water after a short walk, the same systems responding together.

**Combination angle:** build capabilities and apply them to familiar circumstances; the same circulation supports limb, lung, kidney and skin. The growing body becomes the workpiece on which later relationships operate.

**Worthwhile angle:** effortless-seeming acts depend on continuous work within us. An ordinary minute becomes more intelligible, without manufacturing a crisis or medical diagnosis.

**Risk:** goals could tell the player every answer, while each organ arrives exactly when needed. The everyday ending may be too weak to justify a long sequence; challenge-response steps may rename an already functioning body.

## Round 2: where does the player discover a relationship?

All three candidates below reconstruct a simplified physiological model; assembly order is neither embryology nor evolution. They use ordinary compatible-pair hints, no timers or simulated resource quantities. The stated results must carry the consequences in text. These are operation sketches, not final executable recipes.

### D — Three crossings

Begin with blood, a heart, vessels and exchange surfaces. Assemble a circuit, then branch its interfaces toward air, food and waste. Air enters lungs but oxygen must cross into moving blood. Food enters a gut but absorbed molecules must cross into the internal environment. Kidney filtration sends useful substances out with wastes; tubules return much of what the body needs. Connect the interfaces into one body, then follow a drink of water through the system.

Representative work: heart + blood vessels → circuit; circuit + blood → circulation; lung air sacs + pulmonary capillaries → gas-exchange interface; gut lining + intestinal capillaries → absorption interface; kidney filter + recovery tubule → nephron. Specialized interfaces join the continuing circulation rather than creating independent bodies. Each module includes its ordinary supporting anatomy, described concretely.

**Combination angle:** one circulation joins three distinct boundary crossings; output reveals which substances move where. **Worthwhile angle:** the body's outline is not the boundary that matters to its cells. **Risk:** modules supplied ready-made could make the opening small set misleading. Three rounds of “interface + circulation” might become the same action. A drink at the end could be a weak demonstration unless it changes a previously constructed state.

### E — Save the useful things

Start with a blood supply, a pressure-driven capillary filter, a tubule and transport cells. Build a kidney model that initially filters small molecules indiscriminately, then recovers glucose, salts and water into blood. Join its output to a complete circulation/respiration model, add a sensor-to-hormone-to-tubule relationship, and contrast ordinary conditions with water lost through sweating. End with the body conserving water while still excreting wastes.

Representative work: blood supply + glomerular filter → filtrate in a nephron; filtrate-bearing tubule + recovery transport → selective return; water-loss condition + body model → concentrated-body-fluid state; sensed concentration + hormone pathway → increased water recovery. The final states are observations of the same model, not new organs. Supporting kidney gradients and energy supply would be provided explicitly.

**Combination angle:** reverse an apparently sensible first solution—discard everything small—by adding selective return and adjustable recovery. **Worthwhile angle:** human survival depends on continuous discrimination, not simply eliminating “bad things”; water conservation connects kidneys to skin. **Risk:** starting with kidney details may be too technical and late body assembly may feel bolted on. A hormone represented as a tool could disguise a substantial causal chain. This is a kidney story unless wider integration earns its cost.

### F — Build, then meet the weather

Start with a pump, blood, vessels and a thin exchange surface. Build oxygen delivery and nutrient absorption, then a kidney and a skin surface. Converge them into a resting body model. Connect temperature sensing to a control centre and from it to sweat glands and skin vessels. A warm afternoon increases heat gain; the completed connections cause heat loss but also water loss. Connect concentration sensing and antidiuretic hormone to kidney water recovery. End with drinking and recovery rather than an invulnerable “finished” body.

Representative work: ventilated lung surface + circulation → oxygen-delivering circuit; intestinal absorption + circuit → fed tissues; kidney recovery + circulation → recovered solutes; temperature sensor + controller → temperature feedback; feedback + skin effectors → heat-loss response; sweating body + dry conditions → water deficit; kidney regulation + water deficit → water-conserving body.

**Combination angle:** constructions later act together under a specific condition, and one useful response changes what another system must do. **Worthwhile angle:** stability is ongoing work with costs and limits; the environment remains essential. **Risk:** arbitrary environmental-condition tiles could become test buttons. “Fed tissues” and “water-conserving body” must change what can happen next, rather than merely award labels. The feedback stages could repeat an obvious wiring gesture.

## Round 3: three complete prototype scopes

The setting is an explanatory reconstruction of a living body, not surgery or development. Binary combinations connect structures or apply an external condition to the assembled model. Results are authored text, with no live fluid simulation. The intended audience has studied biology. A few → many → one progression is desirable; it does not justify false two-ingredient organ recipes. All candidates end with one integrated body model, not a claim to have fabricated a person.

### G — A glass of water

Three or four short chapters, roughly 12–18 combinations. Begin with blood supply, a capillary filter, a nephron tubule and selective transport. Construct filtration, then recovery: useful glucose appears in filtrate before transport returns it to blood. Construct water recovery from a concentrated kidney medulla and a collecting duct whose permeability can change; connect it to the earlier nephron. Assemble concentration sensing, the hypothalamus/posterior-pituitary ADH pathway and the collecting duct response. Bring gut absorption, skin water loss and the circulating tissues into one body model. Apply substantial sweating without drinking: urine becomes more concentrated but the body remains short of water. Absorb a drink, then use the restored condition to reduce the conservation signal. The final achievement is continuing exchange and adjustment.

**Combination angle:** a small collection branches into filtering, recovering and regulating structures, then joins the body; later inputs expose what those constructions can and cannot accomplish. **Worthwhile angle:** much kidney work is returning useful things; compensation cannot replace a missing supply. Sweat, urine and thirst concern the same internal water. **Hook:** the apparently wasteful filter, the cost of successful cooling, then the limits of conservation. **Risk:** this is a complete physiological episode, not complete anatomy. ADH and medullary gradients could bury the accessible insight; the body may arrive too nearly complete.

### H — The cost of cooling

Four short chapters, roughly 16–22 combinations. Build a thin exchange bed and a thick protective surface, then connect the protective surface to blood vessels, sweat glands and temperature sensing. Assemble lung and intestinal exchange around a common circulation. Join the structures into a body that can cool through evaporation. A warm walk with sweat loss exceeding intake reveals a water deficit. Assemble kidney filtration, selective recovery and concentration-controlled ADH signalling to conserve water; a drink finally replaces the deficit. Optional humid-air comparison shows that sweat can cost water while providing less cooling.

**Combination angle:** a response that solves one problem alters another system's working conditions. Different structures reuse interfaces and flow, eventually becoming one model. **Worthwhile angle:** homeostasis has costs and environmental limits; an organ's function cannot be understood alone. **Hook:** cooling succeeds, but the story is not finished. **Risk:** too many supplied organ modules; false equivalence of skin, lung and gut surfaces; humidity as an optional observation may steal attention from assembly. The renal work could be a late technical detour.

### I — Three doors into one body

Six short chapters, roughly 24–32 combinations. Start with blood, pumping chambers, vessels and a living tissue bed. Assemble a closed circuit serving cells. Independently construct ventilation plus a gas-exchange surface, digestion plus absorption, and kidney filtration plus recovery. Connect all three to the same circulation. Add skin and two specific regulatory paths: temperature sensing changes sweat/skin blood flow; fluid concentration changes kidney water recovery. A warm walk and a subsequent drink make those previously separate constructions cooperate, reducing the board to one responding body.

**Combination angle:** familiar separate systems converge through one circulation and later alter one another's conditions. **Worthwhile angle:** the protected environment around cells depends on several controlled crossings; ordinary acts recruit a whole body. **Hook:** each crossing offers a different surprise, culminating in coordinated response. **Risk:** a dozen preliminary organ fittings could be an entrance fee for the interesting last third. The first circuit may already be familiar to the audience, and supplied special parts weaken the promise of making everything from a few basics. A correct body catalogue may still be tedious.

## School-level reset: three candidates

The user rejected the kidney prototype as too advanced and requested a grades 8–10 organ/system approach. The following comparison supersedes the earlier selection as active direction.

### A — Lift a glass

Start with arm bones, a joint and two opposing muscles. Construct an arm that can bend and straighten; the useful discovery is that muscles pull rather than push. Independently connect heart/blood vessels and lungs/breathing muscles. Add a gut supplying nutrients and kidneys removing wastes. Join these supply routes to the earlier arm, then connect brain, nerves and eyes so it can act on what it sees. Skin adds a temperature response. After a warm period of activity, the completed body reaches for a glass and drinks: sensing, movement, blood flow, fuel, oxygen and exchange now serve one familiar act.

Around 16–18 actions in five small chapters. Early demonstrations establish mechanical possibilities, not isolated organs living without support. Ordinary unmodelled supporting anatomy is present. Detailed endocrine, renal and molecular mechanisms are outside scope.

Worthwhile: ordinary actions depend on many systems; the body's apparent independence relies on continuous internal cooperation and external supplies. Hook: progressively making a familiar body capable of something, with immediate arm movement before wider assembly. Risk: numerous organ hookups may become a checklist, and the ending could merely certify what the audience already expects.

### B — Keep going

Start with bones, joints and muscles, then build circulation and breathing to support activity. A short walk reveals that moving muscles need supplies and produce heat. Attach the gut to replenish supplies over time, kidneys to remove wastes, and skin/control connections to lose heat. The end is one body continuing to work through changing conditions.

Around 12–16 actions in four chapters. Regulation is shown through changes in breathing, heartbeat and sweat, without named hormones or cellular chemistry. No resource depletion meters or artificial emergencies.

Worthwhile: life involves continuous adjustment, and solving one need can create another. Hook: a construction is tested, then improved for a concrete reason. Risk: the model may appear able to walk before the player assembles the neural/energy support it needed; adding each missing organ after a problem appears could become contrived repair. It has less emphasis on sensory coordination.

### C — From a sandwich to a hand

Start with food, stomach and intestines. Build digestion and absorption, circulation, and lungs supplying oxygen. Deliver both nutrients and oxygen to a muscle attached to an arm. Connect an opposing muscle, then nerves and senses, until the body can lift its next bite. Finish with one body whose visible movement is connected to earlier invisible supply work.

Around 12–16 actions in four chapters. Explain stored fuel so a fresh bite is not portrayed as instantly powering the next movement. No ATP, digestive enzymes by name, glucose cotransport or detailed kidney physiology.

Worthwhile: food, air and movement belong to one connected process rather than separate textbook chapters. Hook: a familiar meal acquires an unfamiliar causal story. Risk: it can become a guided tour of food's journey, with early digestion postponing construction of the body; the cyclical ending might be a neat illustration rather than a reason to play.
