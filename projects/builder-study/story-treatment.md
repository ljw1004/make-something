# The Next Box — The Helping Hand

Current narrative recommendation under the [single brief](../../stories/builder/design-brief.md),
following the [concept redevelopment](narrative-redevelopment.md). This is a written
treatment, not an implemented revision or a finding of child enjoyment. The live
game remains v48. The [city-rescue candidate](city-rescue-treatment.md) is superseded.

## Story

Someone sends us wonderful things to make. A small red mechanical hand reaches
through the delivery opening: it catches an escaped ball, returns it, and helps
put a finished toy on our shelf. Once it lets our printing press stamp a pattern
onto one finger. It becomes a recognizable presence with which we share our work.

The boxes become extraordinary. We build little tunnels that send a marble across
an impossible distance and back, a walking machine that climbs the delivery frame,
and a marble track that works upside down. Each gets its own working demonstration.
The hand plays along. Through the tunnels we see it preparing and stamping the next
box: our helper is also our supplier.

Then it puts the latest toy away as usual, slides its fork under the whole loaded
shelf, closes its fingers and takes everything. Cutting power stops it beyond our
reach, still holding the shelf. Through the opening we see a vast collection. Our
things are installed as another exhibit. Beyond the hand's controls sits its owner.
A familiar act of putting things away has become an act of possession.

We build a workshop robot around our wall walker. It can grip, release fittings
and drive another mechanism through a physical coupling. It climbs out along the
arm, disconnects the collector's drive and connects its own. The same red hand
brings our shelf home, this time under our machine's control. The robot disables
its reach and bars the delivery shutters with a brace taken from the arm.

The collector operates its controls. Nothing moves. Our robot fits wheels onto a
small wooden body on our table. We have kept the extraordinary technology and made
a machine that can make more things. The shelf bears a visible repair strap. The
printer's pattern remains on the disconnected finger beside it.

## Supplier and reveal

Privately, the supplier is an acquisitive collector operating a kit company as a
front for a collection of functioning worlds. It wants their inventions and,
eventually, their complete working environments. The kits cultivate workshops at
the sources and provide access. The collector enjoys playing with the makers, but
regards that pleasure as an entitlement to keep their work and their worlds.

Its earlier affection need not have been false. Its limit is revealed when the
maker's wishes conflict with its appetite. Possession supplies the visible motive;
do not substitute a desire for credit, which a portrait cannot establish unaided.

The shelf theft and cabinet communicate the immediate conflict in written readings.
The larger ambition to collect worlds remains author-level intent until actual
storyboards establish it. A diagram of our house could simply mean a model-house
kit; it does not prove the real house is next. Do not claim that inference succeeded
or repair it with an explanatory speech. The next visual test must decide whether
the cabinet can reveal the larger scheme economically or whether to keep the
story's claim at the concrete scale of the theft we actually see.

## Seven builds and their independent rewards

| Build | Literal construction | What it does without the plot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pinball | Board, rebound rails, spring launcher, separate flippers and linkages. | Shoots, rebounds and bats a ball back into play. |
| Printing press | Base, guided platen, lever, linkage, relief plate and paper feed. | Repeated impressions accumulate on paper: a machine makes pictures. |
| Mechanical hand | Palm, distinct fingers, joints, connected tendons and crank. | Curls around counters, lifts and stacks small blocks. |
| Window tunnels | Two mouths, supports, retained pink collars and return ramp on a compact base. | A marble travels through a distant space and comes back; another hand joins the trick. |
| Wall walker | Compact body, alternating foot linkage, motor, blue battery and pink pads. | Walks up and underneath a metal frame while carrying a load. |
| Marble skyway | Compact rigid loops, driven lifting wheel, catching cup, return trough and pink track inserts. | Marbles climb, run beneath the track, drop into a cup and circulate again. |
| Workshop robot | The actual walker, tool saddle, pinch jaws and independently powered shaft-driver arm. | Climbs to inaccessible places, handles fittings, drives mechanisms and assembles another toy. |

Three ordinary builds precede three extraordinary toys and a fourth extraordinary
completed machine made by upgrading one of them. Reuse preserves real dimensions.
The press's prescribed design is not player-authored art; these demonstrations do
not promise steering, composition or other minigames absent from the engine.

The skyway replaces a display carousel. Packaging existing toys for the plot did
not supply a strong enough new construction. Theft now follows completion of a
worthwhile toy; the toy needs no hidden malfunction. The helper causes the betrayal.

## Visible sequence

1. Establish worktable, broad delivery frame and shelf. Let each ordinary build
   work. The hand returns an escaped pinball, helps shelve completed things, and
   receives the printer's ink pattern on its fingertip.
2. The Window tunnels show both ends of an exchange. The marked hand returns the
   ball, stamps the next kit box and sends it toward our delivery opening.
3. Give the walker an unhurried overhead walk and load-carrying demonstration.
   Later it reseats a slipped drive coupling on the red arm. A ratchet holds the
   arm's block while its motor is disconnected: an observable physical fact that
   also changes the meaning of our earlier help when the hand steals the shelf.
4. Complete the skyway and show a full working cycle. The hand shelves it. The
   delivery shutters open fully; the fork slides under the whole shelf, retaining
   fingers close and the arm withdraws it.
5. Disconnect arm power. Motion stops; the mechanical grip keeps the shelf. Follow
   the shelf into the cabinet, locate the owner at the controls, and return to the
   empty shelf brackets in our room. No speech explains the scheme.
6. Set the next sealed kit aside. The new target picture shows the recognizable
   walker with tools. The game still gives clear steps: this is the character's
   rejection of the supplier's programme, not evidence that the player invented an
   unsignposted solution independently.
7. Build the workshop robot. Its demonstration establishes gripping and driving a
   stopped mechanism before the rescue. It climbs the arm, removes the collector's
   drive coupling once and connects its own motor. It returns the supported load,
   then opens the grip only when the home ledge supports the shelf.
8. Clear the arm and robot from the shutter path, disable remote drive, and slide
   the existing doors shut. Drop a detachable rigid arm brace across their handles;
   its captive pins retain it. The robot and shelf remain on our side. Far controls
   now do nothing. End with the robot fitting real wheels to a wooden body.

These are storyboard directions, not player-facing prose. No explanation of pink
parts, congratulation, moral lesson, apology or reconciliation scene is needed.

## Physical staging hypothesis

The theft cannot depend on a scale change. Provisional dimensions are a 1.0 m wide
by 0.65 m clear opening, normally partly shuttered for a 0.35 m crate; a 0.8 m wide
by 0.35 m deep shelf-tray; and loaded height below 0.5 m. Show the full aperture
before tray passage. These are proposed envelopes, not validated arrangements of
the actual models. Change the layout if they do not fit; never resize objects
between shots. Other completed toys and the walker can remain on the worktable.

Keep the shelf on the arm's rigid fork throughout travel, with positive finger
retention. Do not transfer the load onto a tiny robot saddle. Separate service
sockets drive carriage travel and retaining fingers. The robot uses its tool on
one socket at a time; locomotion has a separate motor and blue battery. There is
no unnecessary wrist operation. Return the carriage with fork upright and grip
closed. Only after arrival does the finger mechanism visibly lift its ratchet and
open. No single shaft silently commands unrelated movements.

Removing arm power stops the theft, not the independently powered robot or the
pink passage. Its replacement drive bypasses the collector's disconnected input.
Keep this separation visually obvious. Disconnect the collector's coupling once.
Do not add a redundant second isolation step after the rescue.

Use the existing shutters, not a newly improvised full grille. Before closure,
show the passage clear of fork, carriage, robot, rail obstruction and cables.
The crossbar brace, handles and captive pins require dimensioned modelling; do
not conjure matching holes in an animation. Retain the finger marked by our press
as a recognizable piece of the disconnected mechanism, not an unrelated trophy.

## Evidence and next discriminator

Fresh written-scene readers recognized the helpful hand becoming a collector's
instrument, the shelf theft and the use of acquired knowledge to reclaim it. They
rejected the carousel's weak independent appeal and exposed incompatible hatch
scale, unclear load transfer and unexplained control of multiple axes. A separate
informed reviewer accepted the corrected physical outline as a hypothesis, then
removed a duplicated disconnection and unnecessary wrist operation. It required
visible ratchet release, separate power and an unobstructed closure. Those narrow
corrections are incorporated above; the resulting storyboard has not been tested.

Neither concept reading nor a working animation establishes attachment to a toy.
The user's enjoyment of earlier builds supports the construction mechanic, not
this new roster. The collector's grander motive also remains a visual question.

Prototype theft, drive takeover, safe return and the robot's first act of making
as a rough silent sequence at phone size. Separately test whether each toy's
working result merits its assembly. Reject or revise the premise if the sequence
cannot carry its meaning with minimal text. No replacement manuscript or final
artwork is claimed; native 3D and new minigames remain separate decisions.
